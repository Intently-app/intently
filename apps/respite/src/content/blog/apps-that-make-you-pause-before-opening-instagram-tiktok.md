Most of the scrolling that people regret is not a decision. It is a thumb that opens Instagram while the brain is somewhere else. The fix that works for many people is not a wall but a speed bump: a few seconds between the tap and the feed, enough to ask "do I actually want this?". Here are the apps that do that, how they differ, and how to set one up so it lasts longer than a fortnight.

We make Respite, which is in this list, so we say where the others are better. Details are from the apps' own pages and public listings, checked November 2026; prices are as listed at the time of writing and vary by country.

## What does a pause app actually do?

It intercepts the opening of an app you chose and puts something in front of it for a moment: a breath, a question, a countdown, a prompt. Then you either go in or you do not. Unlike a blocker, it never says no. The theory is simple and well-trodden: habit loops depend on the step between cue and action being frictionless, so adding a small step lets the slower, deliberate part of your mind weigh in. [The science of friction](/blog/science-of-friction/) covers it properly, and [mindful interventions versus app blocking](/blog/mindful-tech-interventions-vs-blocking/) explains when each is the better tool.

## How do the main three differ?

**one sec** is the best known and the most researched. It reports about a 57 percent reduction in opens of targeted apps in a study it cites; the attribution of that study has been disputed, so treat it as the company's figure rather than settled science. It offers several intervention types and, per its App Store notes, an on-device reflection step. It is free for one app and Pro is around $20 a year. If breathing exercises are what you want, it is the benchmark. See [Respite vs One Sec](/blog/respite-vs-one-sec/) and [one sec alternatives](/blog/one-sec-alternatives/).

**ScreenZen** is simple and free: a delay before the app opens, plus stats on how often the delay turned you away. If you want the minimum, it is a good minimum. See [Respite vs ScreenZen](/blog/respite-vs-screenzen/).

**Respite** adds a short pause and then everything around it: usage tracking, per-app limits, schedules, streaks and a free game layer (acorns, quests, a weekly chest), plus sync across iPhone, Android and Chrome with Respite Plus. On Android and Chrome the pause rotates reflection questions, a short breathing exercise, your own usage stats and activity suggestions; on iPhone the pause is Apple's Screen Time shield. You cannot write your own pause messages. It is free to start, and Plus adds sync and bring your own AI, where Claude or ChatGPT can read your usage to help you tune it ([how that works](/ai/)).

## What's the right way to set up a pause?

1. **Pick one or two apps.** The ones you open without deciding. Not your whole home screen, or you will tap through all of it within a week.
2. **Choose when it appears.** A pause on every open treats a reflex habit; a pause at a limit treats a time habit. Many people want both: the reflex catch, and a daily stop.
3. **Add a daily limit.** A pause you can always tap through is a nudge, not a stop. A limit gives the nudge somewhere to land. [How to reduce Instagram usage](/blog/reduce-instagram-usage/) and [how to stop TikTok scrolling](/blog/stop-tiktok-scrolling/) have app-specific plans.
4. **Keep it short.** Five seconds, not thirty. Annoyance makes people delete the app.
5. **Review weekly.** Opens and minutes, not feelings. If you are tapping through every time, shorten the list or tighten the limit.

If you use an AI, a weekly check is a good fit: connect Claude or ChatGPT to your usage and ask how often you walked away from the pause and for which apps. The [30 prompts](/blog/chatgpt-claude-screen-time-prompts/) post has the exact wording.

## Does the pause work the same on iPhone and Android?

Not quite, and it affects which app suits you. On iPhone, apps are built on Apple's Screen Time framework, which keeps the choice of apps private and lets an app place a shield in front of them; what the shield can show is limited, so pauses on iPhone tend to be simpler. On Android, an app can show richer screens, which is why Android pauses often include breathing animations, questions and usage stats. If you use both, pick an app that exists on both and keeps your settings in step; Respite does this with Respite Plus sync, and one sec has an Android version. The browser is the third place people scroll, and a pause there (Respite has a Chrome extension) closes the loophole of opening Instagram in a tab instead.

## What makes a pause stop working?

Three things, mostly: it is too long, so you resent it; it is always the same, so you stop reading it; or it guards too many apps, so it turns into noise. The fixes are the reverse. Keep it to a few seconds, choose a tool that varies what it shows, and guard only the apps that matter. If you notice yourself tapping through on autopilot, treat that as information: add a daily limit, or move the app behind a schedule for the hours when you are most vulnerable, usually late evening. [Why willpower fails](/blog/why-willpower-fails-phone-addiction/) explains why designing the situation beats trying harder.

## Is a pause better than blocking?

For autopilot habits, often yes; for hard deadlines, no. A pause keeps you in charge, which is why people tolerate it long enough for it to work. A block removes choice, which is why people bypass it. Many sensible setups use both: a pause for everyday life, and a block or a locked schedule for exam week. See [do app blockers actually work?](/blog/do-app-blockers-work/) for the evidence and [how to schedule app blocking](/blog/schedule-app-blocking-iphone-android/) for the how-to.

## What about iPhone's built-in options?

Apple's Screen Time can limit an app's daily time and schedule downtime, but it does not pause every open, and the Ignore Limit button is easy to tap. It is a useful free baseline, and [how to block apps on iPhone](/blog/how-to-block-apps-iphone/) walks through it. A pause app adds the moment of friction Screen Time lacks.
