Your teenager's phone holds their friends, their homework, their music and most of their jokes, so "less screen time" can sound a lot like "less life". Rules handed down from above get argued with, worked around or quietly ignored. The ones that last look different: there are fewer of them, each has a reason, and your teen helped write them.

This guide covers which rules are worth having, how to agree them without a standoff, and what to do the first time one breaks.

## Why do teenagers ignore screen time rules?

Mostly for the same reasons adults do:

- **The rule doesn't make sense to them.** "Two hours a day" means little when a homework video, a call with a friend and a gaming session all count the same.
- **There are too many rules.** A long list becomes a long list of loopholes.
- **They had no say.** Testing limits is part of growing up, and a rule nobody asked them about is one worth testing.
- **The rule only applies to them.** Teens notice when "no phones at dinner" has an exception for a parent's work email.
- **Breaking it costs too much.** If one slip means losing the phone for a week, hiding the slip is the rational move.

None of this means teens can't keep rules. It means the rules have to be built for the person following them, which is also why [willpower alone rarely fixes phone habits](/blog/why-willpower-fails-phone-addiction/).

## What makes a screen time rule stick?

Put a rule that tends to fail next to one that tends to hold, and the difference is easy to see:

| Tends to fail | Tends to hold |
|---|---|
| "Less phone time." | "Phones charge in the kitchen from 10 pm on school nights." |
| Announced by a parent at dinner | Proposed by either side, agreed together |
| Applies to the teen only | Applies to everyone it reasonably can |
| No reason given | One sentence on why: sleep, school or family time |
| Lasts forever | Reviewed after a month |
| Broken rule means the phone is confiscated | Broken rule means a reset and a short talk |

Specific beats vague, few beats many, and shared beats imposed. It also helps to know that the American Academy of Pediatrics stopped giving a fixed hour count for older children in 2016 and suggests families make their own media plan. There is no magic number, so you're making one that fits your house. For the age-by-age picture, see [how much screen time is too much](/blog/how-much-screen-time-is-too-much/).

## Which screen time rules for teenagers are worth having?

You don't need all of these. Pick three to five that match where the friction actually is in your house.

1. **Phones charge outside bedrooms overnight.** If you keep only one rule, keep this one. A phone by the bed is hard for anyone to ignore, and sleep is where late-night use does its most obvious damage. A cheap alarm clock settles the "I need it to wake up" argument. See our guide to [stopping scrolling at night](/blog/stop-scrolling-at-night/) for more.
2. **No phones at meals.** Simple, visible, and easy to apply to the adults as well.
3. **Homework with the phone out of reach.** Let your teen choose the version: phone in another room, or a short break with it between subjects. Our guide to [stopping phone use in class](/blog/how-to-stop-using-phone-in-class/) has a setup that works at home too.
4. **Your teen picks one or two apps to cut back on, and how.** This is the rule that is most theirs: a daily limit on a short-video app, a pause before a feed, or deleting a game during exams. Their choice, their reasons.
5. **A notifications rule.** Group chats muted during homework and after a set time at night. Fewer buzzes, fewer "just checking" moments.
6. **A few safety rules that aren't up for debate.** Not sharing location with strangers, and telling you if something online feels wrong. Say plainly that these are fixed, and why.

Notice what's missing: a total daily number. A cap can be part of the deal if your teen wants one, but sleep, meals and homework usually matter more than the total.

## How do you agree the rules with your teen?

Treat it as a meeting, not a lecture. A rough running order:

1. **Pick a calm moment,** not the evening after an argument about the phone.
2. **Ask what bugs them about their own phone use first.** Plenty of teens can name something: the late nights, the homework that drags, the feed they open without meaning to. Their complaint is a better starting point than yours.
3. **Share your concerns briefly, with reasons.** "I care about your sleep" lands better than "you're on it too much".
4. **Draft three to five rules together.** Either side can propose one. Anything one of you strongly objects to goes back on the table.
5. **Agree what happens when a rule slips,** before it does.
6. **Write it down and add a review date.** A note on the fridge or a pinned family message is enough. A month is a good first review.

Here's a hypothetical example. Sam, 15, hates how late he finishes homework; his parents care about his sleep. They agree on three rules: phones in the kitchen from 10:30 on school nights (parents included), group chats muted during homework, and a daily limit on one video app that Sam picks and sets himself. At the review, Sam asks to move the Friday cutoff to 11. That's the review doing its job.

## Should you use monitoring apps on a teenager's phone?

Parental controls and spyware-style monitoring are not the same thing. Built-in tools such as Apple Screen Time with Family Sharing and Google Family Link let a parent set limits in the open, and they're a sensible backstop for a fixed rule like the overnight cutoff. Our guide to [screen time for parents](/blog/screen-time-for-parents/) walks through both, including Time Allowances and the Screen Time Schedule (App Limits and Downtime on iOS 26 and earlier).

Covert monitoring is a different thing. Reading messages or tracking every tap behind a teen's back tends to teach them to hide their phone use rather than think about it, and when they find out, trust takes a hit no rule can repair. If you do use oversight, tell your teen what it is and why, and plan to loosen it as they show they can manage. In a few years nobody else will be setting their limits.

## What should happen when a rule gets broken?

It will get broken, probably in the first week. Plan for that:

- **Treat the first slip as information.** Was the rule unclear, too strict, or just hard on a bad day?
- **Reset rather than punish.** "Phone back in the kitchen tonight" works better than a week without it, which mostly teaches better hiding.
- **Look at the rule, not just the teen.** If the same rule keeps breaking, change it at the next review.
- **Keep your side of the deal.** If your phone came to dinner, say so. Owning your slips makes the agreement more believable.

If one rule really does need firmer enforcement, a built-in control can back it up, as long as your teen knows it's there.

## When is it about more than screen time?

Sometimes heavy phone use is a symptom rather than the problem. If you notice lasting low mood, withdrawal from friends, a sudden drop at school or sleep that is regularly wrecked, talk with your teen and consider their doctor or a school counsellor.
